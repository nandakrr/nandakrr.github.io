🏢 How Europeans communicate at work

  • Be direct. "I don't agree, because..." is normal and respected. Long, polite introductions can sound unclear.
  • "No" is OK. Saying "yes" and then not delivering is much worse than an honest "I can't do that by Friday."
  • Flat hierarchy. Use first names, including your manager's. Say "Hi Anna", not "Respected Ma'am" or "Sir".
  • Be on time, especially in Germany, the Netherlands and the Nordic countries. Joining 5 minutes late is rude.
  • Don't over-promise. "I'll try" means "probably not". Give realistic dates.
  • Work–life balance is real. Evening or weekend messages are often not expected — and not answered.
  • Small talk is short. Weekend, weather, holidays. Avoid salary, religion and marriage questions.

    🆓 Free resources (no paid tutor needed)

    Talk to real people for free

    • Tandem or HelloTalk (free apps): language exchange. You help someone learn Hindi or your native language, and they help you with English. Many partners are Europeans. Do voice calls, not just chat.
    • Meetup.com or local groups: search "English conversation" or "language exchange" in your city.
    • At work: ask one colleague with good English to talk only in English with you over lunch, once a week.

    Listening (YouTube / podcasts, free)

    • BBC Learning English: 6 Minute English. Start here: short, clear, with useful words.
    • TED Talks. Turn on English subtitles, then try without them.
    • Rachel's English (YouTube): American pronunciation, slowed down and explained.
    • Series: watch with English subtitles, never Hindi. Good to start with: Friends, The Office, Brooklyn Nine-Nine.

    IELTS (free official material)

    • IELTS official website and the British Council have free sample speaking questions and band descriptors.
    • IELTS Liz (website and YouTube): free speaking tips and model answers.

    How to actually get better

    1. Speak a little every day

    15 minutes daily is better than 2 hours on Sunday. Keep your streak alive.

    2. Slow down

    Good speakers talk at about 120–160 words per minute. Speaking fast when nervous makes you harder to understand. Slow is confident.

    3. Replace "um" with a pause

    A 1-second silence sounds thoughtful. "Um, uh, basically, actually" sound unsure. Notice them in your feedback and cut one at a time.

    4. Use a structure

    PREP: Point → Reason → Example → Point.
    For updates: What I did → What's next → Any blockers.
    For problems: What happened → Impact → What we're doing.

    5. Short sentences

    One idea per sentence. You don't need complex grammar to sound professional.

    6. Record and re-say

    After each session, write a better version and say it again. That second attempt is where learning happens.

    7. Shadow native speakers

    Pick a 2-minute clip (TED talk, podcast, YouTube). Play a sentence, pause, copy it exactly — speed, stress, tone.

    8. Real conversations

    Once a week, speak up once in a meeting on purpose — even just "Can I add one thing?" Use the phrases tab to prepare.
